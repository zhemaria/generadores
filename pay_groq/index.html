Why the question generator needs a paid Groq plan

What the free tier can and cannot do, what the paid plan actually costs, and how the billing works — so the department can decide

The short version

  • The MyOpenMath generator works on the free plan for arithmetic, algebra and numerical statistics, mostly as multiple choice.
  • It cannot produce anything with a coordinate plane, a line graph, a scatter plot or a geometric figure with axes. Those requests are refused before they are sent.
  • This is a throughput limit, not a quality limit. The paid plan removes it completely.
  • There is no subscription and no monthly fee. You are billed per token used.
  • Measured cost: about $0.19 per 100 questions. A full 200-question course bank costs roughly 40 cents.

Everything below is measured from this tool against Groq's own API, not estimated.

1

Why this tool is unusually demanding

Most AI tools send the model a short instruction: “write five questions about fractions.” This one cannot. MyOpenMath questions are not plain text — they are algorithmic code. Every number in the question is generated at random when a student opens it, so thirty students get thirty different versions of the same exercise, and the answer key is computed rather than stored.

Writing that code correctly requires the model to follow a long list of rules about the IMathAS language MyOpenMath uses: which functions exist, which loop syntax is legal, how to keep a fraction from coming out as 6/8, how to avoid a division by zero on the one random draw in fifty that would produce it, how to label a figure so the values are not blank. Each of those rules exists because it was a real bug in a real classroom.

That rulebook travels with every single request. The model has no memory between questions. So a request that produces one exercise carries roughly 4,400 to 6,900 tokens of rules before the model writes a single character.

2

What the free plan allows, and where it stops

Groq's free plan does not cap how much you can use in a month. It caps how much you can send in any one minute. For the models currently available, that ceiling is 8,000 tokens per minute — and it counts the request and the reply together.

Compare that to the table above and the arithmetic is unforgiving: a 6,894-token request plus a 2,450-token answer is 9,344 against a ceiling of 8,000. It does not fit, and no amount of waiting helps, because the problem is the size of a single request rather than the total for the day.

One more practical limit

The ceiling is a rolling minute, so even a request that fits will fail if you generate two in quick succession. On the free plan you can produce roughly one question per minute. Building a thirty-question bank means sitting there for half an hour.

A second constraint is worth naming plainly: Groq's catalogue changes. The models this tool originally recommended were retired in June 2026 and replaced by ones with a smaller per-minute allowance. The free plan is a place to evaluate a tool, not a foundation to build a course on.

3

What the paid plan costs

Groq's paid tier is called Developer. It raises the per-minute ceiling from 8,000 tokens to roughly 250,000–300,000, which removes this constraint entirely: every question type in the table above becomes available, and you can generate continuously instead of once a minute.

Pricing for the model this tool uses, GPT-OSS 120B, is $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output tokens. Applying those rates to the measured request sizes gives the real cost of a question:

What that means in practice

You would have to generate 519 questions to spend one dollar. A 200-question bank covering a full course costs about 38 cents. A department of six instructors each building a bank per term would spend somewhere in the region of two to three dollars a year.

Two discounts apply automatically and lower this further. Groq caches repeated prompt content and charges half rate on cache hits — and this tool sends the same rulebook every time, so the cache hit rate is high. Choosing the smaller GPT-OSS 20B model instead halves the rates again. The 200-question bank drops to about 31 cents with caching, or 20 cents on the smaller model.

4

How the billing actually works

This is the part worth understanding before anyone approves anything, because it does not work like a software subscription.

  • No monthly fee and no seats. There is no per-user licence and no minimum. You are charged only for tokens actually used.
  • Nothing changes about the tool. It is the same file, the same key field, the same buttons. Upgrading only changes the rate limits attached to the account.
  • Usage is charged to a payment method on the account. Groq's console shows spending as it accrues, so there are no surprises at month end.
  • Limits apply per organization, not per key. If several instructors share one departmental account, they share the 250,000-token minute — which is still around thirty questions per minute across the whole department.
  • The free plan does not disappear. An instructor who does not need graphing questions can stay on it indefinitely at zero cost.

Two ways to organise it

One departmental account. A single key, one small charge, everyone shares the allowance. Simplest to approve and to track, and the per-minute ceiling is generous enough that instructors will not notice each other.

Individual accounts. Each instructor holds their own key and their own (tiny) bill. More administrative work for an amount of money that rounds to nothing, but it keeps usage clearly attributable.

Whichever route is chosen, the sum involved is small enough that the real decision is not financial. It is whether the department wants graphing and coordinate-plane questions in its MyOpenMath banks. If the answer is no, the free plan is genuinely sufficient and nothing needs to be spent.

5

The honest case against paying

A recommendation is worth less if it only argues one side, so here is the case for doing nothing.

  • Most of a GED math course does not need graphs. Percent, fractions, ratios, equations, area and volume, mean and median — all of that already works for free. If your banks are mostly computational, paying buys you convenience rather than capability.
  • The free plan's real cost is your time, not money. One question per minute is slow, but a thirty-question bank is half an hour of background work you can do while grading.
  • Graphing questions can come from elsewhere. MyOpenMath's shared library already contains thousands of graphing items written by other instructors, and they are free.
  • Any external service can change. Groq retired two models in June 2026 with a few weeks' notice. Building a workflow that depends on one vendor carries a maintenance cost that a two-dollar bill does not capture.

The case for paying is narrower than the price suggests: it is worth it if you specifically want algorithmic, auto-graded, randomized graphing questions in your own words, and want them quickly. If that describes your course, forty cents is not a decision. If it does not, stay on the free plan with a clear conscience.

6

Where these numbers come from

The token counts are not estimates. The generator asks Groq how large each request actually was and stores the answer, and it reads each model's real per-minute ceiling from Groq's own response headers rather than from anything written into the code. If you open the tool and press the ruler button beside the model list, it will measure your account and show you the same table for your own key.

The prices are Groq's published rates for GPT-OSS 120B as of August 2026. Rates and rate limits both change; before committing anything, confirm the current figures at groq.com/pricing and console.groq.com/docs/rate-limits.

If anything here does not match what you see on your own account, please say so — the numbers in this document are only useful if they are current.
